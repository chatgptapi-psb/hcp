Elevating Team Leadership Through Coaching and Conflict Resolution

Author: Michael Storer

Effective leadership in the home services industry is the cornerstone of a successful business, whether in plumbing, HVAC, or cleaning services. A thriving team requires owners to master two essential skills: coaching and conflict resolution. This guide breaks down these key components, focusing on coaching principles and best practices for handling conflicts. By integrating these skills, you can inspire your team, improve performance, resolve conflicts with empathy, and create a positive environment that fosters accountability and growth.

Coaching Principles for Employee Development

Coaching is a process that supports growth by focusing on future development, not just correcting past mistakes. Effective coaching can transform how employees see themselves and their roles, leading to stronger performance and deeper engagement. Let’s break down the core principles of a coaching conversation:

1. Set Clear Goals
Start by defining the purpose of the conversation. Understand what the employee and the business want to achieve. Whether it’s a goal related to performance, leadership development, or improving customer service, clarity is key. Example Question to Ask:
​“Where do you see yourself growing within the company in the next six months?”
2. Identify Strengths and Opportunities
Help the employee recognize their strengths and areas for improvement. By doing so, you not only encourage self-awareness but also create a dialogue about how their strengths can be leveraged to address weaknesses. Example Question:
​“What do you believe are your current strengths in your role, and what areas do you think need improvement?”
3. Create an Action Plan
Once you’ve identified the areas for improvement, develop an action plan. This plan should be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound (SMART). By laying out clear steps and setting milestones, you give your employees a clear path to success. Example:
If you’re grooming someone for a leadership role, a solid plan might include mentoring sessions, specific training on leadership skills, or shadowing a current manager. Regular check-ins help keep them accountable and ensure steady progress.
4. Evaluate Progress
Regularly review the employee’s progress toward the goals you’ve set together. Adjust the plan as needed based on real-time feedback and observations. Providing constructive feedback along the way ensures the employee stays on course and knows where they stand. Example Question:
​“What challenges have you encountered so far, and how can we adjust our approach to help you succeed?”

Best Practices for Conflict Resolution

In any team, conflict is inevitable, but how it’s handled will determine whether it weakens or strengthens the team. Avoiding conflict can lead to more significant problems, disengagement, and even loss of employees. On the other hand, addressing conflict effectively helps build trust and cooperation.

There are two types of conflict: destructive and constructive. Destructive conflict focuses on blame, personal attacks, or avoidance. Constructive conflict, on the other hand, aims to resolve disagreements by focusing on the issue and finding solutions.

1. Address Conflict Immediately

One of the biggest leadership mistakes is ignoring conflicts, hoping they will resolve themselves. Confront issues as soon as they arise, focusing on behaviors and solutions, not the individuals involved.

Best Practice:
Start with a neutral, non-accusatory tone. For example: “Can you help me understand your approach in this situation?”

This opens up a discussion without placing blame and encourages the other person to reflect on their actions.

2. Lead with Empathy

Remember that conflicts often arise from misunderstandings or external stressors. When you approach conflict with empathy, you show that you value the person and not just the result.

Tip:
Listen carefully and validate their feelings before proposing a solution. This helps to lower defenses and foster open dialogue.

3. Establish Boundaries

Set clear boundaries for acceptable behavior. Conflict resolution must be focused on solutions, not personal attacks or blame. Ensure that everyone knows that discussions must remain respectful, and address the issue, not the individual.

Tip:
Establish ground rules for how conflicts should be handled in the workplace, including rules against personal attacks and unprofessional behavior.

4. Create a Safe Space for Open Communication

Encourage your employees to come forward with issues without fear of retaliation. An environment where employees feel safe to express their concerns is one where conflicts can be addressed quickly and constructively.

5. Document and Follow Up

After resolving a conflict, document the conversation and any action steps decided upon. Regular follow-up ensures that the resolution is working and that the behavior or issue doesn't resurface.

Example:
If an employee was spoken to about tardiness, follow up after two weeks to see if the issue has been resolved and provide feedback on their progress.

Practical Tips for Applying Coaching and Conflict Resolution

- Prepare Thoroughly: Whether it’s a coaching session or conflict resolution, take time to prepare by outlining your objectives and questions. This ensures a focused and productive conversation.
- Encourage Dialogue: Use open-ended questions to guide the conversation and help the employee come to their own realizations. This fosters ownership and accountability.
- Remain Neutral in Conflict: When handling disputes, remain calm and focus on solutions, not assigning blame. This approach helps de-escalate tensions and promotes problem-solving.
- Follow Up Regularly: Whether coaching for development or resolving conflict, following up demonstrates that you are committed to continuous improvement and accountability.

Conclusion

Mastering coaching and conflict resolution is key to building a strong, high-performing team. By implementing the coaching principles outlined above, you can empower your employees to develop their skills and reach their full potential. At the same time, fostering a culture of constructive conflict resolution ensures that disagreements are opportunities for growth, not barriers to progress. Both approaches will lead to a more engaged workforce and better business outcomes.
